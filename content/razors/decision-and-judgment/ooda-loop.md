## Statement

Observe, orient, decide, act — and win by cycling faster than the situation changes, not by
deciding better.

## In plain terms

The **OODA loop** says that in a changing situation, the speed of your decision cycle matters more
than the quality of any individual decision. A merely adequate decision made now, against current
information, beats a good one made against information that is three days old. Most of the
advantage comes from getting through the loop again sooner.

## Decides

Whether to invest in deciding better or in deciding sooner, when conditions are changing
underneath you.

## Why it holds

Any decision is made against a snapshot, and the snapshot decays. In a fast-moving situation the
world has moved by the time a carefully-analysed decision is executed, so the decision is optimal
for a state that no longer exists.

Speed also compounds. Each cycle produces information — the action generates a result you can
observe — so a team running four cycles has four rounds of evidence where a team running one has a
prediction. That is a different quality of knowledge, not merely more of it.

Boyd's argument is stronger than "be fast": operating inside an opponent's cycle means they are
continually responding to a situation you have already changed, which produces confusion rather
than merely delay.

The step he considered decisive is **orient** — how you interpret what you see, shaped by prior
experience and existing models. Two teams observing the same data reach different decisions
because they orient differently, and orientation is the part that is hardest to change under
pressure.

## Example

Two teams respond to a competitor launching a feature that is taking their customers.

The first commissions a proper analysis. Market research, a customer survey, a design phase, and
an eight-week build — a considered response, launched in fourteen weeks, against a market that has
had fourteen weeks to settle around the competitor's version.

The second ships a rough version in nine days, watches what customers actually do with it, and
ships four more revisions over the following six weeks. Three of the four are wrong, and each one
produces information the survey could not: which part people use, what they ask for next, which
assumption was mistaken.

At week fourteen the second team has a feature shaped by five rounds of real feedback. The first
has a well-researched feature shaped by predictions made three months earlier.

The individual decisions in the second team were worse. The loop was faster, and that turned out
to dominate.

## Limits

It is a razor for changing, contested situations. Where conditions are stable and the decision is
expensive to reverse, deliberation wins — a schema migration or an external API is not improved by
cycling faster.

Fast loops also require cheap actions. If each cycle costs a quarter and cannot be undone, you do
not have a loop, you have a sequence of commitments — and speed there is recklessness with a
framework attached.

And the framework is routinely reduced to "move fast", which drops the orient step that Boyd
considered the whole point. A fast loop with bad orientation converges quickly on the wrong thing.

## Source

Boyd developed OODA as a fighter pilot and strategist, presenting it in briefings rather than
published papers — his "Patterns of Conflict" ran for hours and was never written up as a book.

The popular version has flattened it into a four-box cycle. Osinga's 2007 study is the corrective:
Boyd's own diagram shows orientation feeding back into observation, with cultural traditions,
previous experience and new information all shaping it, and the loop is far less linear than the
summary suggests.
