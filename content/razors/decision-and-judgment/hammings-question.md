## Statement

What are the important problems in your field, and why are you not working on them?

## In plain terms

**Hamming's question** is uncomfortable by design. Most people work on what arrived — the ticket,
the request, the thing that was already in progress — and never separately ask what actually
matters. The gap between those two lists is usually large, and nothing in an ordinary week will
surface it.

## Decides

What to work on, at the scale of a quarter or a career, rather than what to do next.

## Why it holds

Work arrives with its own momentum. Tickets, requests and inherited projects fill the available
time completely, and none of them carries a label saying whether it is among the important
problems — so the question never gets asked unless someone asks it deliberately.

The importance distribution is also extremely skewed. A small number of problems account for most
of the available impact, and effort spread across whatever arrived is mostly spent outside them.

Hamming's stronger claim is about compounding. Working on important problems builds relevant
knowledge, relevant reputation and access to further important problems, so the choice is not a
one-off allocation — it changes what will be available to you next.

And the second half of the question is where the value is. Everyone can name the important
problems; the useful part is the honest answer to why you are not working on them, which is
usually that they are hard, unassigned, or would require a conversation you have not had.

## Example

A staff engineer's quarter is full: three design reviews a week, an escalation rotation, two
migrations, and a steady flow of questions only they can answer. Every item is real work and the
quarter is genuinely productive.

Asked the question directly, they can name the important problems in about a minute. The
reconciliation data is untrustworthy and nobody owns it. Nobody can say what the system costs per
customer. The team's onboarding takes four months.

Asked why they are not working on any of them, the honest answers are specific: the first belongs
to no team, the second requires asking finance for data, the third is nobody's job.

None of those is a reason. They are descriptions of the fact that unassigned work does not arrive
in a queue, which is precisely why the important problems stay unworked — and why the person with
cross-team scope is the one who has to notice.

## Limits

It is a question about direction and it is not a licence to abandon commitments. Someone who
answers it by dropping their obligations has traded a real cost for a speculative one.

The framing is also individual, and much of what matters is not individually choosable. Someone
early in their career, or in a team with a fixed mandate, does not have free choice over their
problems — and the answer "because I have not been given the scope" is honest rather than evasive.

And importance is not self-evident. Hamming's field had reasonably legible open problems;
"important" in a product organisation is contested, and the question can be used to justify
whatever the asker already wanted to work on.

## Source

Hamming delivered "You and Your Research" as a Bell Labs colloquium in March 1986, drawing on
thirty years of working alongside people who did and did not produce significant work. The talk is
mostly about the difference between them, and the question is its central device.

He describes asking it at lunch, repeatedly, to colleagues who stopped inviting him — which he
reports without much regret, and which is a fair indication of how the question lands.
