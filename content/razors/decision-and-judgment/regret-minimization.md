## Statement

For decisions at the scale of years, choose what you would least regret at eighty rather than what
optimises now.

## In plain terms

**Regret minimisation** is a way of evaluating large, rare decisions — a job, a move, a
foundational technical bet — by projecting forward to an age where the short-term costs have
stopped mattering. It reliably changes the answer, because the things that dominate the decision
now are usually the things that fade fastest.

## Decides

Career-scale and life-scale choices: whether to take the role, make the move, start the thing,
leave.

## Why it holds

The short-term costs of a large change are vivid and specific — the salary gap, the disruption,
the awkward conversation — while the long-term value is abstract and uncertain. So the immediate
calculation systematically over-weights the near side.

Projecting to eighty reverses the asymmetry deliberately. From there the six uncomfortable months
are invisible, and what remains visible is whether the thing was attempted, which is the variable
you were under-weighting.

The empirical asymmetry supports it. Research on regret consistently finds that regrets of
inaction outlast regrets of action: things done badly are reinterpreted, absorbed and explained,
while things not attempted stay open-ended and are revisited indefinitely.

It also filters correctly by scale. Most decisions produce no regret at eighty in either
direction, and the framing says so immediately — which is itself useful, because it identifies
which decisions do not deserve this much deliberation.

## Example

An engineer is offered a role at a small company doing work they find genuinely interesting, at a
30% pay cut, requiring a move.

The near-term calculation is unambiguous and negative. The pay gap is a number, the move is
concrete and disruptive, the company might fail, and the current job is fine. Every specific cost
is on one side of the ledger.

The projection changes what is being compared. At eighty, a 30% pay difference across two years is
not a fact that anyone remembers. Whether they spent their thirties on work they found
interesting, or spent them somewhere fine, is.

That does not automatically decide it. Someone with a mortgage they cannot service at the lower
salary has a real constraint, and the framing does not remove it — but it does clarify that the
constraint is the actual reason, rather than letting the vivid short-term costs do the deciding
while pretending to be an argument.

## Limits

It is only for large, rare, hard-to-reverse decisions. Applied to ordinary choices it is both
unhelpful and paralysing — most of what you decide this year does not register at eighty, and
demanding that it justify itself at that scale is a way of making small decisions enormous.

It also systematically favours action, which is usually right and not always. "I would regret not
trying" is a sentence that can be produced for almost any risk, including ones with consequences
that cannot be recovered from.

And it can be used to ignore obligations that are not yours alone. A decision affecting other
people is not adequately evaluated by projecting only your own eventual regret.

## Source

Bezos has described the framework repeatedly as the tool that decided his leaving a stable job to
start Amazon in 1994 — projecting to eighty, he found the salary and the bonus invisible, and the
attempt visible.

The underlying psychology is better documented than the anecdote. Kahneman and Tversky's work
established regret as a distinct and powerful factor in decision-making, and later studies of
long-term regret consistently find the inaction asymmetry — which is what gives the framework its
predictable direction.
