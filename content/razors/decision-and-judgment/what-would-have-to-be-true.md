## Statement

Stop arguing about which option is right and ask what the world would have to look like for each
to be right.

## In plain terms

**What would have to be true** converts a disagreement about preferences into a list of
conditions. Instead of debating whether to build or buy, each side states what would have to hold
for their option to be the correct one — and those conditions can usually be checked. The argument
becomes a small piece of research.

## Decides

How to resolve a disagreement between two defensible options where neither side is moving.

## Why it holds

Positional arguments have no stopping rule. Each side restates its case with more emphasis, both
become more confident, and the outcome is decided by seniority or stamina rather than by anything
about the options.

The reframe works because people can articulate the conditions for their own position much more
easily than they can defend the position itself. "This is right if traffic stays under 500
requests a second" is a sentence the advocate will happily produce, and it is falsifiable in a way
the advocacy was not.

Most of the conditions then turn out to be checkable. Growth rates, request volumes, how many
callers exist, what the contract actually says — the disagreement was frequently about a fact
nobody had looked up, and an afternoon of measurement settles what a month of meetings could not.

It also makes losing survivable. An option ruled out by a condition failing was not rejected by a
person, which is what allows the advocate to drop it without cost.

## Example

Two engineers disagree about whether to build a scheduling component or adopt an off-the-shelf
one. Three meetings produce the same two positions restated with increasing conviction.

The question changes the shape immediately. For *buy* to be right: the vendor has to support
per-tenant isolation, cost has to stay under about £40k a year at projected volume, and the
migration path has to be under six weeks. For *build* to be right: the scheduling requirements
have to be genuinely unusual, and the team has to have capacity for ongoing ownership.

Four of those five are answerable in a day. The vendor supports isolation, the cost lands near
£25k, the migration is quoted at four weeks — and the requirements turn out to be entirely
ordinary once written down.

Nobody had to be persuaded. Two of the build conditions failed, the advocate could see that
themselves, and the decision took an afternoon of checking rather than a fourth meeting.

## Limits

It needs conditions that are actually checkable. Where both sides' conditions are unfalsifiable —
"if the team culture supports it", "if we stay disciplined" — the reframe has moved the argument
without resolving it.

It also does not settle genuine value disagreements. When one person weights speed and another
weights safety, both sets of conditions can be satisfied simultaneously, and the honest conclusion
is that this needs a decider rather than more evidence.

And it can be used dishonestly, by stating conditions you know are unfalsifiable or by choosing
conditions your option trivially satisfies. The defence is having each side state conditions for
the *other* option as well.

## Source

Martin and Lafley present it in *Playing to Win* as a strategic-choice technique, developed at
Procter & Gamble as reverse-engineering: rather than evaluating options against evidence, articulate
what each option requires and then test the requirements.

Martin's 2010 article makes the argument for why it works — that people defend positions and
examine conditions, and the switch removes most of the ego from a choice.
