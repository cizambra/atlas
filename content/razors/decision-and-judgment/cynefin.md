## Statement

Match the method to the domain: clear, complicated, complex and chaotic problems each need a
different move.

## In plain terms

**Cynefin** separates four kinds of problem. Some have a known right answer, some need an expert
to work one out, some can only be understood by trying something and watching, and some need you
to act immediately and think afterwards. Applying the wrong method is a common and expensive
mistake — and the most common is analysing a complex problem as if it were merely complicated.

## Decides

What approach to take when you do not yet know what kind of problem you have.

## Why it holds

The four domains differ in whether cause and effect can be known in advance, and that is what
determines which method can work at all.

In the **clear domain** the relationship is obvious to anyone: sense, categorise, respond. Best
practice exists and following it is correct. In the **complicated domain** the relationship is
knowable but requires expertise: sense, analyse, respond. Good practice exists and analysis finds
it.

In the **complex domain** cause and effect are only visible in retrospect, so no amount of analysis
produces the answer in advance. The move is probe, sense, respond — run a safe-to-fail experiment
and see what happens. In the **chaotic domain** there is no discernible relationship at all: act,
sense, respond, to establish enough stability to move somewhere else.

The reason it matters is that using analysis on a complex problem produces a confident plan built
on a causal model that does not exist. Most large organisational and product failures are that
mistake.

## Example

A team is asked why customer retention dropped four points last quarter, and produces a six-week
analysis: cohort breakdowns, funnel metrics, a regression, and a plan built on the strongest
correlation.

The plan does not work. Retention moves another point in the wrong direction, and the correlation
that drove the plan does not reproduce in the next quarter.

The problem was complex, not complicated. Customer behaviour is a system with feedback, competing
products, and thousands of individual reasons — cause and effect are visible in retrospect and not
in advance, so the analysis produced a plausible model of something that has no stable model.

The domain-appropriate move is a set of small parallel experiments — three changes, each
safe-to-fail, each measured — accepting that two will teach nothing and one may reveal a
mechanism. It feels less rigorous than the six-week analysis and it is the only approach that can
actually find the answer.

## Limits

The boundaries are not sharp, and arguing about which domain a problem sits in can consume more
time than any of the methods. The framework is a prompt for choosing an approach, not a
classification scheme to be defended.

It also gets used as a label rather than a decision. Calling something "complex" and continuing to
plan it in detail is worse than not having the vocabulary, because the word now provides cover.

And most real work spans domains. A single project usually contains clear parts, complicated parts
and complex parts, and the useful application is per-decision rather than per-project.

## Source

Snowden developed Cynefin — Welsh for "habitat" — while at IBM in the late 1990s, and the 2007
*Harvard Business Review* article with Mary Boone is the most cited presentation of it. The
framework has been revised repeatedly since, including renaming the "simple" domain to "clear" and
adding a central state of confusion for problems whose domain is not yet known.
