## Statement

What you already spent is not a reason to continue; only the remaining cost and benefit are.

## In plain terms

A **sunk cost** is money, time or effort you cannot get back whatever you decide next. It feels
like a reason to keep going — six months in, stopping means wasting six months — and it is not.
The only question that has an answer is whether finishing is worth what finishing still costs.

## Decides

Whether to continue, change course or stop, on any project where significant effort has already
been spent.

## Why it holds

The decision is entirely about the future. Two options are available from where you stand now,
each with a remaining cost and a remaining benefit, and what was spent getting here is identical
in both — so it cannot distinguish them.

The reason it feels otherwise is that stopping makes the loss legible. Continue, and the six
months is still notionally an investment; stop, and it becomes a visible write-off with a name
attached. The **sunk cost fallacy** is largely the avoidance of that moment.

Escalation of commitment is the organisational version, and it is stronger. Staw's finding is that
the people who authorised the original decision invest *more* after negative feedback, not less,
because reversal is an admission — which is why the person who started a project is the worst
person to decide whether it should continue.

## Example

A rewrite is eighteen months in. The original estimate was nine, roughly 60% of the functionality
is migrated, and the remaining 40% is the awkward part — the undocumented integrations and the
reporting nobody understands.

An honest re-estimate says twelve more months. The argument in the room is that stopping now
wastes a year and a half of work.

The question that actually decides it is different: is twelve more months the best available use
of that team, starting today? Against the alternatives — a targeted fix to the three specific
problems the rewrite was meant to solve, at maybe three months — the answer is probably no.

And the eighteen months are gone under both options. They are not recovered by finishing; they are
simply not part of the comparison. The uncomfortable version is that a project can be 60% complete
and still be the wrong thing to finish.

## Limits

It does not say past investment is irrelevant to everything. It is evidence about your estimating,
about the domain's difficulty, and about how much remains — the same information that produces the
forward-looking estimate.

There are also real forward costs to stopping that people mislabel as sunk. Reputational damage, a
partner commitment, morale, and the cost of a team learning that work gets abandoned — these are
future consequences and belong in the comparison.

And the fallacy has a mirror. Abandoning things at the first difficulty is its own failure, and
"sunk cost" can be used to justify never finishing anything hard. The razor argues for evaluating
the remaining work honestly, in both directions.

## Source

Arkes and Blumer's 1985 experiments established the effect cleanly, including the theatre-ticket
studies where people attend an event they no longer want because they paid for the ticket.

Staw's earlier work on escalation of commitment is the organisational half, and the more useful
one here: his finding is that decision-makers responsible for an initial failing investment
allocate *more* to it after negative results than uninvolved people do, which is why the
separation of the decider from the original author matters more than any amount of discipline.
