## Statement

People rise to their level of incompetence, because promotion rewards the previous job rather than
the next one.

## In plain terms

The **Peter principle** says that if promotion is based on performance in the current role, people
keep being promoted until they reach one they are not good at — and then stop. The mechanism is
not about individuals; it is about what the promotion decision measures.

## Decides

What to evaluate when promoting someone, and how to structure a ladder.

## Why it holds

The premise is that adjacent roles require different skills. Being an excellent engineer and being
an excellent engineering manager share very little, and being an excellent senior engineer is not
the same as being an excellent staff engineer — one is measured by difficulty, the other by scope.

If the promotion signal is performance in the *current* role, it is a weak predictor of performance
in the next one. Promotion continues while performance is strong, and stops when it is not, which
places people at the first role they are not good at and leaves them there.

The empirical support arrived fifty years after the book. Benson, Li and Shue analysed sales
promotions across 214 firms and found that the best salespeople were far more likely to be promoted
to management and performed measurably worse as managers — the effect the principle predicts, in
data.

The correction follows from the diagnosis. Evaluate against the *next* role rather than the current
one, which usually means the person has to have been operating at that level before the title — the
[[operating at the level]] argument — and provide a technical ladder so competence at building is
not routed through a management role.

## Example

The strongest engineer on a team is promoted to team lead. The reasoning is legible and universal:
they are the most technically capable, the most respected, and the most productive.

Six months later the team is worse off. The lead is still doing the hardest technical work because
they are fastest at it, one-on-ones are cancelled for delivery pressure, two engineers have not
had feedback in a quarter, and the hiring loop has stalled.

None of that is a character failure. The role is people, process and prioritisation, and none of
those skills was assessed — they were promoted on a signal that measured something else entirely.

The organisation also lost its best engineer, twice over: they are now spending 70% of their time
on work they were never assessed for, and their technical output has fallen accordingly.

The version that avoids it is a real technical ladder with staff-level scope, and a management
promotion assessed on evidence of managing — mentoring, running a project, handling a difficult
conversation — before the title rather than after.

## Limits

It assumes skills do not transfer and people do not learn, and both are too strong. People grow
into roles routinely, and a good manager is frequently someone who was promoted before they were
ready and supported through it.

Peter's book was also satire. It reads as management theory and was written as comic sociology,
which is worth knowing before citing it as an established finding — the serious evidence is the
2019 paper, not the 1969 book.

And it describes a failure of promotion *criteria* rather than of people. Framing an individual as
"at their level of incompetence" is both unkind and a misreading — the principle indicts the
process that put them there.

## Source

Peter and Hull published *The Peter Principle* in 1969 as a humorous treatise on hierarchiology,
and it became a bestseller largely because the observation landed harder than the joke.

Benson, Li and Shue's 2019 study is the serious version: using data on over 50,000 sales workers,
they show firms promote on current-role performance despite it predicting worse managerial
performance, and estimate the cost of doing so.
