## Statement

Give one person outside the hiring team a veto, so urgency cannot quietly lower the standard.

## In plain terms

A **bar raiser** is a trained interviewer from outside the hiring team, with a **hiring veto**, whose
job is to protect the long-term standard rather than to fill the role. The mechanism exists because
the hiring manager's incentives and the company's diverge exactly when the team is most desperate.

## Decides

Who has final say on a hire, and how to stop urgency from eroding standards.

## Why it holds

The incentive misalignment is structural rather than a matter of discipline. A hiring manager with
an unfilled role, a delivery commitment and a tired team wants someone competent now; the company
wants someone who raises the average, and those diverge under pressure.

Nothing about that is a character flaw. The manager's costs are immediate and concrete, the
company's are diffuse and delayed, and every individual decision to accept a borderline candidate is
defensible.

The bar raiser's independence is what corrects it. They carry none of the delivery pressure, they
are assessed on hiring quality rather than on filling roles, and they can say no — which is why the
veto rather than an opinion is the mechanism.

The standard framing matters too. Amazon's version asks whether this candidate is better than
roughly half the people currently at that level, which is an explicit ratchet: a bar that only moves
up, rather than a fixed threshold that drifts.

The training is not incidental. Bar raisers are calibrated interviewers who have done many loops,
which is what makes the veto credible rather than arbitrary — an untrained outsider with a veto is
noise with authority.

## Example

A team has been trying to fill a senior role for four months. The last two candidates declined, a
project is slipping, and everyone is covering.

A candidate comes through who is solid — competent, would contribute, and is clearly not stronger
than the median person on the team. Every person on the loop leans yes, and the reasons are all
about the team's situation rather than the candidate.

The bar raiser, who has never met this team and has no stake in the project, says no. Their reading
is that this is a fine hire for a team that is not desperate and a below-median one for this level,
and the four-month pressure is doing the deciding.

That is unpopular and correct. The role is filled six weeks later by someone stronger, and the six
weeks cost less than a below-bar senior hire would have cost over three years.

The mechanism worked precisely because the person exercising it did not care about the six weeks. A
process where the hiring manager weighs their own urgency against a standard produces the standard
losing, reliably, and no amount of intending otherwise changes it.

## Limits

It costs time and trained people. Every loop needs a bar raiser who is not on the team, and
maintaining a calibrated pool is a real investment that small organisations cannot make.

It also slows hiring, which is sometimes the wrong trade. A startup that needs someone now may
correctly decide that speed beats the marginal quality difference, and the mechanism is not free.

And the veto can be misused. A bar raiser who blocks candidates for stylistic reasons, or who
applies a personal standard rather than a calibrated one, is a bottleneck with authority — which is
why the calibration and the training are the load-bearing parts rather than the veto itself.

## Source

Amazon introduced the programme in 1999, during rapid growth, specifically to prevent the standard
from falling as hiring volume rose — the observation being that fast-growing companies dilute
themselves by hiring for immediate need.

Bryar and Carr's account is the most detailed public description, including the training process and
the explicit "better than half the current people at this level" formulation that makes the bar a
ratchet.
