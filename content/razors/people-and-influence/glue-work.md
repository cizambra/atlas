## Statement

The invisible coordination that holds projects together is essential, uncredited, and a career built
only on it stalls.

## In plain terms

**Glue work** is the noticing and connecting that makes projects succeed — writing the document
nobody owns, onboarding the new person, spotting the gap between two teams, unblocking the thing
that would have stalled. It is genuinely necessary, it is largely invisible to promotion processes,
and it is disproportionately done by women and underrepresented engineers.

## Decides

How much glue to do, and what to do about the fact that it is uncredited.

## Why it holds

Glue work is real work that produces no artifact. Preventing a problem, connecting two people who
needed to talk, or noticing that nobody owns a piece — none of these generates a commit, a design
document or anything a promotion committee can evaluate.

The distribution is the part Reilly's essay is actually about. The work gets done by whoever notices
it needs doing, which selects for people who are attentive to the team's state — and that attention
is not evenly distributed, so the same people absorb it repeatedly.

The career consequence is structural rather than a matter of unfairness in any single decision.
Promotion at senior levels is assessed on technical impact and scope, so an engineer whose year was
spent on coordination has a year that is invisible in exactly the terms the committee uses.

Reilly's conclusion is the one that gets dropped from the summary. It is not "stop doing glue work",
which would be worse for everyone — it is that a career built only on glue stalls, so it has to be
either bounded, credited, or paired with visible technical work.

The organisational version is that this is an [[opportunity allocation]] failure. The engineer
carrying the glue has no capacity for the visible project, which is why the same people keep getting
both kinds of work in the same direction.

## Example

An engineer notices that a cross-team project has no shared timeline, so they make one. Then they
notice the two teams disagree about an interface, so they set up the conversation. Then they onboard
the new joiner because nobody else has time.

Over a year they do this continuously, and the projects they touch succeed noticeably more often
than the ones they do not.

At promotion time the packet is thin. There is no design document with their name on it, no service
they own, no migration they led — because the year was spent on the connective work that made other
people's projects land.

The committee's assessment is that they are a strong senior engineer with unclear technical scope,
which is an accurate reading of the evidence presented and a poor reading of the year.

The version that works changes two things rather than the work ethic. The glue that only their
cross-team context makes possible is kept and made visible — written up, credited, described in
outcome terms. The glue anyone could do is rotated, automated, or explicitly assigned, which frees
capacity for one piece of visible technical scope.

## Limits

It is not an argument for refusing coordination work. A team where nobody does glue fails in exactly
the ways the essay describes, and the engineer who stops doing it entirely has offloaded a cost onto
colleagues.

The framing also does not fix the underlying process. An organisation whose promotion criteria
cannot see coordination work has a criteria problem, and individual advice about visibility is a
workaround rather than a solution.

And "glue" gets used loosely for any non-coding work, which dilutes it. Technical leadership,
architecture and mentoring are legible and creditable; the essay is about the work that is genuinely
invisible.

## Source

Reilly's 2019 talk and essay is the canonical statement, framed around a hypothetical engineer whose
manager tells her she is not technical enough for promotion after a year of doing exactly the work
that made the team successful.

*The Staff Engineer's Path* develops it further, and the practical advice is the useful part: name
the work, negotiate what you will and will not do, make the essential glue visible, and ensure the
year contains something a committee can evaluate.
