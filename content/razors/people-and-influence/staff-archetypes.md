## Statement

Tech lead, architect, solver, right hand — four shapes of the same level, and organisations need
different ones.

## In plain terms

Larson's four archetypes describe what staff engineering actually looks like in practice. A **tech
lead** guides one team's execution; an **architect** owns direction in a critical area across teams;
a **solver** is dropped into whatever the hardest current problem is; a **right hand** operates with
a senior leader's authority. Same level, very different jobs.

## Decides

Which shape of staff work a company needs, and whether it matches what you are good at.

## Why it holds

The four differ in scope, horizon and what they optimise, so they are not interchangeable.

**Tech lead** — one team, quarters, execution. The most common entry point and the closest to a
senior role, usually paired with an engineering manager. The characteristic failure is remaining a
very good senior engineer with a new title.

**Architect** — one critical area, years, direction. Depth rather than breadth, and it requires an
organisation that has long horizons, which many do not. The failure is drifting away from what the
code actually does.

**Solver** — whatever is worst right now, weeks to months, containment. Valued where there are
recurring fires. The failure is never staying anywhere long enough for anything to compound.

**Right hand** — organisational problems at a leader's scope. The rarest, the least like
engineering, and the failure is spending the technical credibility the role rests on.

The reason the taxonomy is useful is diagnostic rather than descriptive. An organisation needs one
of these at any given moment, and a mismatch reads to everyone — including the engineer — as
underperformance rather than as a fit problem.

An architect in a company that needs solvers is seen as slow and theoretical. A solver where an
architect is needed is seen as unfocused. Neither is a capability question, and neither is
diagnosable without the vocabulary.

## Example

An engineer strong at deep systems work over long horizons takes a staff role at a fast-growing
startup.

The company needs solvers. Something is on fire most weeks, horizons are measured in sprints, and
the valued behaviour is arriving at a problem and containing it quickly.

Their instinct is to build a coherent direction: understand the whole system, write the strategy,
sequence the work. Six weeks in, the feedback is that they are slow to deliver and too focused on
long-term concerns.

Both readings are accurate. The company genuinely does need someone to stop the fires, and the
engineer genuinely is good at something else — and without the archetype vocabulary the conversation
becomes about performance.

With it, the conversation is available: this company needs a solver right now, that is not what I am
best at, and the options are that I adapt deliberately, that the role is reshaped, or that this is
the wrong fit. All three are better outcomes than a year of mutual disappointment.

## Limits

The four are not a personality test, and most people move between them across a career. Treating
your archetype as fixed is a misreading — Larson's framing is about what the organisation needs at a
moment.

The categories also blur in practice. Most real staff roles are combinations, and a strict
classification is less useful than the underlying question of what shape of work this company is
actually asking for.

And the taxonomy comes from a specific slice of the industry — mid-to-large technology companies
with a defined staff level. In organisations without one, or in very small companies, the
distinctions describe less.

## Source

Larson developed the archetypes from the interviews collected at staffeng.com, where the pattern
emerged from repeatedly finding that people with the same title described substantially different
jobs.

The collection is the more useful artifact than the taxonomy: the narratives make clear how much of
the role is determined by company stage, culture and what problem happened to be pressing.
