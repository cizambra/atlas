## Statement

Match direction to competence and confidence: the same support that helps a novice insults an
expert.

## In plain terms

How much direction someone needs is a property of the person *and the task together*, not of the
person. Grove's term is **task-relevant maturity**: a strong senior engineer running their first
cross-team migration needs close support, and the same person running their third needs none.
Calibrating to the person rather than the task is the common error.

## Decides

How much structure, check-in and direction to give when handing over a piece of work.

## Why it holds

The two failure directions are both real and both costly. Under-supporting someone on unfamiliar
work looks like abandonment and produces avoidable failure; over-supporting someone competent looks
like distrust and removes the learning that autonomy provides.

Hersey and Blanchard's model varies two things independently — **directive and supportive** behaviour
— across four levels. High direction and low support for a beginner; high both while confidence is
low; high support and low direction as competence arrives; low both once they are running it.

Grove's contribution is the qualifier that makes it usable. Task-relevant maturity is not seniority:
it is specific to this person on this task, and it resets when the task changes. Which is why "they
are senior, they will be fine" is how good engineers get dropped into work they had no way to
succeed at.

The support level also has to move. A handover that started with weekly pairing should be visibly
reducing over a quarter, and if it is not, one of you has become comfortable with an arrangement
that was meant to be temporary.

The cheapest calibration is asking. "How much do you want me involved in this?" is a question most
people answer accurately, and it converts a guess into an agreement you can revisit.

## Example

A capable senior engineer is handed a cross-team capacity review — a task they have never done.

Calibrated to the person, the handover is a document and a deadline. They are senior, this is
analysis, and they will manage. Three weeks later they are stuck, not on the analysis but on why
finance distrusts the Q2 numbers — history that exists in nobody's document.

Calibrated to the task, the handover is different. Task-relevant maturity is low, so the first one
is done together: the model assumptions, the data sources, who has to see it before the VP, and
which constraints are real rather than inherited.

The support then reduces deliberately. The second review is a check-in at the midpoint; the third is
a read of the final draft; the fourth is nothing.

The same person needed close support and then none, and the variable was the task rather than their
ability. Getting that backwards in either direction is the whole failure mode: the first version
abandoned them, and the version where the pairing continues into the fourth quarter would have been
the other error.

## Limits

The empirical support for the specific four-quadrant model is weak. Situational leadership is widely
taught and has repeatedly failed to validate cleanly in research, and the specific prescriptions
should be treated as a vocabulary rather than as findings.

Self-assessed confidence is also unreliable in both directions. Someone overconfident on an
unfamiliar task will decline support they need, and someone capable but anxious will ask for more
than is useful — which is why observed behaviour beats stated preference.

And it can become a justification for micromanagement. "Their task-relevant maturity is low" is
available as a reason for indefinite oversight, and the corrective is that the support level has to
be visibly reducing.

## Source

Hersey and Blanchard developed the model in the late 1960s, and it became one of the most widely
taught leadership frameworks in corporate training — with a research record considerably weaker than
its adoption.

Grove's task-relevant maturity, from *High Output Management*, is the more defensible version for
engineering: same underlying insight, stated as a property of the person-task pair rather than as a
four-box model.
