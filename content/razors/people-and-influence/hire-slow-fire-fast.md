## Statement

A false positive costs a team a year; a false negative costs one good candidate. Optimise
accordingly.

## In plain terms

The **asymmetric hiring cost** argument is that the two errors are not equal. A **false positive
hire** — someone who should not have been hired — costs months of managing, damages the team, and
takes a year to resolve. A false negative costs one good candidate who goes elsewhere. So the
process should be biased toward rejecting.

## Decides

Which way to lean when a hiring decision is genuinely uncertain.

## Why it holds

The costs really are asymmetric, and the size of the gap is what makes it a razor. A wrong hire
consumes management attention, lowers the team's standard, discourages strong people, and is slow
and unpleasant to unwind — six to twelve months is a normal figure.

Rejecting a good candidate costs the recruiting effort and the candidate. That is a real cost and it
is bounded, recoverable, and does not compound.

The [[bar raiser]] mechanism exists because of this asymmetry, and so does the convention that any
strong objection in a hiring loop blocks. Both are ways of making the process default to no when
uncertain.

The second half — fire fast — is the part that gets quoted and misapplied. Its honest form is that a
hire who is not working should be addressed promptly rather than tolerated for a year, because
delay is worse for the person than an early honest conversation.

But the asymmetry is routinely used to justify far more caution than it supports, which is where the
razor needs its own limit. Missed good candidates are invisible — the error is never counted — so a
process tuned entirely against one error type drifts toward rejecting anyone unlike the current
team.

## Example

A hiring loop produces a split: three yes, two on the fence. The candidate is competent, the
interviews were unremarkable, and nobody is enthusiastic.

The team has been short for four months and the temptation is to hire. The reasoning is that the
candidate is fine, the role is open, and waiting costs the team more.

The asymmetry says no. Fine is what an unremarkable loop looks like, the cost of being wrong is a
year, and the cost of waiting six more weeks is six weeks.

What the razor does not license is the version that follows in many organisations, where the same
asymmetry justifies rejecting anyone with an unusual background, a non-linear career, or an
interview style that differs from the team's — because every one of those looks like uncertainty.

The honest application requires the second half. Reject on evidence of a specific gap, not on the
absence of enthusiasm, and check the outcomes: if nobody hired in two years is performing poorly and
the process rejects 95%, the bar is not calibrated, it is drifting upward without feedback.

## Limits

The asymmetry is real and the multiplier is folklore. Nobody has measured "a bad hire costs a year"
in a way that generalises, and the figure is repeated with more confidence than its evidence
supports.

Missed candidates are also not free, and their invisibility is the problem. An organisation that
rejects consistently and never reviews outcomes has no feedback on whether its bar is calibrated,
and the drift is always in one direction.

"Fire fast" is also the most abused half. It reads as licence for quick dismissals, and the
defensible version is prompt honest feedback and a real chance to correct — not speed for its own
sake, which is both unjust and produces worse information.

And the whole framing assumes the interview signal is good. Schmidt and Hunter's evidence is that
unstructured interviews predict poorly, so a process leaning hard on a weak signal is being
confidently wrong in a consistent direction.

## Source

The phrase is widespread hiring folklore with no single origin. Amazon's bar-raiser rationale is the
most explicit institutional version, built directly on the claim that the cost of a below-bar hire
exceeds the cost of a longer search.

The countervailing evidence is worth holding alongside it: Schmidt and Hunter's meta-analysis
establishes how weak most interview signal actually is, which means a strong bias toward rejection
is being applied to a noisy measurement.
