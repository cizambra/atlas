## Statement

Find the currency the other person values and trade in it; authority is one currency among many.

## In plain terms

**Currencies of exchange** is Cohen and Bradford's framing: everyone has things they want and
things they can give, and influence is finding an exchange that works for both. Authority is one
currency and usually not the one available — but recognition, information, resources, access,
autonomy and future support all are.

## Decides

How to get something done by someone who does not report to you.

## Why it holds

The reframe is from persuasion to exchange. Persuasion assumes the other person will act if the
argument is good enough, which is frequently false — a correct argument that costs them a quarter is
still a cost, and correctness does not pay it.

Exchange asks what they get. That question has an answer surprisingly often, and it is usually not
money: a team that adopts your platform gets fewer pages, a manager who lends you an engineer gets
that engineer trained on something valuable, a lead who supports your proposal gets a dependency
they wanted removed.

The currencies Cohen and Bradford enumerate are worth knowing, because engineers reach for one or
two:

- **task-related** — resources, information, technical support
- **position-related** — recognition, visibility, reputation, contacts
- **relationship-related** — acceptance, understanding, personal support
- **inspiration-related** — vision, excellence, moral correctness

Reciprocity is what makes it compound rather than transactional. Cialdini's finding is that people
return favours reliably, and the practical version is to give first: help with something before you
need anything, and the exchange happens later without a negotiation.

The failure mode this replaces is arguing harder. When a proposal is declined and the response is a
better version of the same argument, the diagnosis was that they did not understand — and the actual
reason was usually that nothing in it was for them.

## Example

A platform engineer needs six teams to migrate to a new deployment pipeline. There is no mandate and
no authority.

The argument-based approach is the one that fails. The new pipeline is better — faster, safer, more
consistent — and every team agrees, and none of them prioritises the migration, because it costs
them two days and the benefit is diffuse.

The exchange approach asks what each team wants, and the answers differ. One team is drowning in
on-call noise, so the pitch is the alerting the new pipeline brings by default. One is blocked on a
compliance requirement the new pipeline satisfies. One has a new joiner and wants onboarding to be
simpler.

Two teams have nothing to gain, so the currency is effort: the platform engineer does their
migration for them, which costs a day and buys the adoption.

The last team is genuinely too busy this quarter, and the honest exchange is future support — they
migrate next quarter, and get help with something they care about in return.

Same migration, six different currencies, and none of them was the technical argument that everyone
already agreed with.

## Limits

It can shade into transactionalism. Relationships kept as a ledger are unpleasant and read that way,
and the version that works is closer to generalised reciprocity — help people, expect nothing
specific — than to explicit trade.

Some things have no available currency. A team asked to do work with genuinely nothing in it for
them, on a schedule set by someone else, is being asked for a favour — and naming it as a favour is
more honest than manufacturing an exchange.

And it does not replace authority where authority is appropriate. Some decisions should be mandated,
and spending months building influence for something a director could settle in a sentence is its
own failure.

## Source

Cohen and Bradford developed the framework at Stanford and Babson, and the currencies taxonomy is its
most-used part — a checklist for the question "what could this person possibly want from me".

Cialdini's work supplies the reciprocity mechanism, which is the strongest of his six principles in
organisational settings and the one that makes giving first a strategy rather than altruism.
