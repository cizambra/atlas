## Statement

You earn larger scope by handling problems slightly larger than the ones you were given, visibly.

## In plain terms

**Scope is granted** rather than claimed. You do not get a bigger problem by asking for one or by
being ready for one — you get it by handling something slightly beyond what you were given, well
enough and visibly enough that the next thing gets handed to you. The increment is what makes it
work.

## Decides

How to grow into a larger role rather than waiting to be given one.

## Why it holds

Scope is a bet someone else makes with their own credibility. A manager assigning a cross-team
problem is staking something on the outcome, so the decision is made on evidence of handling
something comparable — and that evidence has to exist before the assignment.

Which produces the ordering people find frustrating: you demonstrate the level, then you get the
title and the scope. Waiting to be given scope so you can demonstrate it is a loop with no entry
point.

The increment is the mechanism. Slightly larger is a bet someone will take; substantially larger is
not, because the downside is a failed project and the evidence does not support it yet. Each
successful increment raises what the next bet can be.

Visibility is the other half, and it is not optional. Scope handled well that nobody outside your
team observed does not compound, because the person making the next assignment did not see it —
which is why the [[the invisibility problem|translation]] work is load-bearing rather than
self-promotional.

The corollary is about where to find the increment. Unowned problems are the cheapest source: the
thing falling between two teams, the migration nobody is leading, the decision that keeps not being
made. Taking one is not asking permission, it is the wedge.

## Example

An engineer wants staff scope. The team's work is well-defined and bounded, and there is no
cross-team project available to lead.

Asking produces the honest answer that there is nothing to give. Waiting produces another year of
the same work, which is the [[the same-year problem|same year repeated]].

The increment is available and not labelled. Two teams have been quietly disagreeing about an
interface for months, nobody owns the decision, and everyone is working around it.

Taking it is not a promotion request. It is a week of conversations, a short document naming the
options and a recommendation, and a decision that finally gets made — slightly beyond their given
scope, and unambiguously useful.

Six weeks later a second cross-team question comes to them, because they are now the person who
handled the last one. Six months later they are leading a migration.

Nothing was granted at the start. The first increment was taken, done well, and made visible — and
every subsequent one was handed over on the strength of the previous.

## Limits

The environment has to have larger scope in it. In a company where every problem fits inside one
team, no amount of handling increments produces staff scope — which is the [[scope ceiling]], and it
is environmental rather than personal.

Taking unowned work also has to be done carefully. Picking up something that belongs to a team you
have no relationship with generates resistance unrelated to the quality of the work, and "unowned"
is a judgment that can be wrong.

And the increment can be too large. Taking a problem substantially beyond your demonstrated range
and failing at it costs more credibility than not taking it — the razor says slightly larger for a
reason.

## Source

Larson's framing in *Staff Engineer* is that scope is one of the things a company grants, alongside
the title and the role, and that the granting follows demonstrated behaviour rather than preceding
it.

Reilly's treatment is more practical about how to find the increment, and the recurring advice is
the same: look for the problems nobody owns, because those are the ones available to take without
anyone having to give them to you.
