## Statement

A mentor talks to you; a sponsor talks about you in rooms you are not in. Only one changes your
trajectory.

## In plain terms

Mentorship is advice — someone gives you their time and experience. Sponsorship is advocacy —
someone spends their credibility to get you an opportunity. The **sponsorship gap** is the finding
that the people who receive the most mentoring frequently receive the least sponsorship, and it
tracks the promotion gap.

## Decides

What to offer when you want to help someone's career, and what to ask for when you want help with
your own.

## Why it holds

The two differ in what is at risk. Mentorship costs the mentor time and nothing else; if the advice
is bad, they are not exposed. Sponsorship means putting your name on someone — recommending them,
arguing for them in calibration, handing them the visible project — and if they fail, it costs you.

That risk is exactly what makes it work. A recommendation that costs the recommender nothing carries
no information; one that stakes their judgment is evidence.

Opportunity is the other asymmetry. Advice improves how someone does the work they already have;
sponsorship changes which work they get. Careers are decided by the second, because promotion
committees assess demonstrated scope and scope is allocated rather than earned in private.

Ibarra's research is the finding that gives the razor force. Women report receiving *more* mentoring
than men and less sponsorship, and the sponsorship differential tracks the advancement differential
— which means the common organisational response to a promotion gap, more mentoring programmes,
addresses the wrong variable.

The practical asymmetry is that mentorship is easy to volunteer for and sponsorship has to be
decided. Nobody accidentally spends their credibility on someone.

## Example

A senior engineer wants to help a promising colleague.

The comfortable version is mentorship. Regular one-to-ones, advice on how to approach problems,
feedback on their designs. Genuinely useful, pleasant for both, and costs an hour a fortnight.

A year later the colleague is a better engineer with the same scope. The advice improved their work;
nothing changed what work they were given.

The sponsorship version costs something. Handing them the cross-team migration that would have been
the senior engineer's — the most visible project available. Saying their name in the staffing
discussion, specifically: "Priya should lead this, she ran the reconciliation fix and finance trusts
her." Arguing for them in calibration against resistance.

And saying "if it slips, that is on me", then holding to it in the retro.

A year later they have led a cross-team migration, which is a promotion case. The mentoring would
never have produced one, however good the advice — because a committee cannot evaluate advice
received.

## Limits

Sponsorship is finite, which mentorship is not. Credibility spent on one person is not available for
another, and sponsoring carelessly makes the next three recommendations harder — so it has to be
allocated rather than distributed.

It also requires the person to be ready in a specific sense: reliably good at what they were last
given, and someone whose success will not cost the sponsor anything to explain.

And the razor is sometimes read as devaluing mentorship, which is wrong. Context transfer —
explaining how the organisation works, what the political map is, what happened in 2022 — is
information people cannot get elsewhere, and it is what makes an opportunity usable when one arrives.

## Source

Ibarra's HBR work is the clearest statement of the gap and its consequences, drawing on research
into why women's greater access to mentoring does not translate into advancement.

Hogan's essay is the most practical on what sponsorship concretely looks like — the specific actions,
ordered roughly by what they cost the sponsor — and it is the one to read if the question is what to
actually do.
