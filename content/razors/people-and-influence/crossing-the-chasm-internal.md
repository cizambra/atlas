## Statement

Internal adoption follows the same curve as products — win the early adopters, then the pragmatists
who want proof.

## In plain terms

An internal platform, standard or practice spreads through an organisation the way a product spreads
through a market. Innovators try it because it is new. **The pragmatist majority** adopts on
evidence, and the evidence they trust is a team like theirs having succeeded — which is why the
**internal adoption curve** stalls exactly where product adoption stalls.

## Decides

Who to target when trying to get an internal tool, standard or practice adopted.

## Why it holds

The groups adopt for different reasons, so the same pitch cannot reach them.

Innovators and early adopters try things because they are new or because they have the problem
acutely. They are cheap to convince and they are not representative — their adoption proves nothing
about whether it works for a normal team, which is exactly what the next group wants to know.

The pragmatist majority is the group that decides whether it becomes the default. They adopt on
evidence, and specifically on evidence from a team like theirs — not on enthusiasm, not on the
architecture, and not on the fact that the platform team believes in it.

Moore's chasm is the gap between those two. Something can have five enthusiastic internal adopters
and stall completely, because the reference that would convince the majority is a boring team
succeeding boringly, and the early adopters are not that.

The late majority moves when not adopting becomes the harder path — the default changed, the tooling
assumes it, the old way warns. Arguing with this group is a poor use of time; changing the defaults
is what moves them.

The practical sequence follows directly: find one team with the problem acutely, make them succeed,
tell that story with numbers, use it to reach the majority, then convert agreement into defaults so
the rest happens without persuasion.

## Example

A platform team builds a service template and gets five adopters in a month — all from the teams who
enjoy new tooling.

Then nothing for a quarter. The platform team's read is that the remaining teams are resistant, and
the response is more evangelism: a tech talk, a wiki page, a Slack campaign.

None of it moves anyone, because the pragmatists are not short of information. They are waiting for
evidence that it works for a team like theirs, and every existing adopter is an enthusiast who would
have tried anything.

What moves it is one ordinary team — busy, sceptical, no particular interest in tooling — being
helped through adoption until they succeed. Their result is the reference: "three days to a
production service became forty minutes, and observability worked on day one."

That story reaches the majority in a way five enthusiasts could not, and adoption resumes.

The last quarter of teams never respond to any of it. They move when the service generator defaults
to the template and the old path emits a warning — which costs no further persuasion and is the only
thing that works on them.

## Limits

The curve is a description of a population rather than a law, and organisational adoption has
dynamics markets do not — mandates, reorganisations and a single influential sceptic can all
override it.

It also assumes the thing is worth adopting. Using the framework to explain slow adoption of
something that is genuinely not better is a way of misreading resistance as a stage.

And chasing the laggards is the specific waste it warns against. They are the loudest objectors and
the last to move regardless, and energy spent arguing with them is energy not spent making the
early adopter's result undeniable.

## Source

Rogers established the adoption categories in 1962 from studies of agricultural innovation, and the
five-group model has held up remarkably well across domains.

Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* added the discontinuity — the gap between early adopters and the
pragmatist majority — which is the part that explains why internal platforms with enthusiastic early
users so often stall.
