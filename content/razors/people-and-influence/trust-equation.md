## Statement

Credibility plus reliability plus intimacy, divided by self-orientation — the denominator is what
most people get wrong.

## In plain terms

The **trust equation** decomposes trust into four components: credibility is whether you know what
you are talking about, reliability is whether you do what you said, and intimacy is whether people
can be candid with you. **Self-orientation** is how much you appear to be in it for yourself — and
because it is the denominator, it can undo all three.

## Decides

What to work on when people do not take your advice, and it is not a competence problem.

## Why it holds

The structure is the argument. Three additive terms and one divisor:

$$
\text{Trust} = \frac{\text{Credibility} + \text{Reliability} + \text{Intimacy}}
{\text{Self-orientation}}
$$

Engineers over-invest in credibility because it is the one they know how to improve. Being more
right is a legible goal, and it is the term with the least remaining headroom for most technically
strong people.

Reliability is unglamorous and moves the number more. Doing what you said, when you said, repeatedly
— it compounds through small instances rather than large ones, and it is the term most damaged by
over-committing.

Intimacy is the willingness to be candid, in both directions. It grows from admitting uncertainty,
saying when you were wrong, and treating what people tell you carefully — and it is the term most
engineers ignore entirely.

Self-orientation is the denominator and it is where the leverage is. Someone who appears to be
optimising for being right, for credit, or for their own agenda is discounted regardless of how high
the numerator gets — which is why a technically excellent engineer can be routinely routed around.

The practical form: reduce self-orientation by making the conversation about their problem rather
than your solution. Asking what they are worried about, crediting other people specifically, and
visibly changing your mind all move the denominator, and none requires being more right.

## Example

A staff engineer is technically the strongest person in the organisation and is repeatedly not
consulted on decisions in their area.

Credibility is not the issue — everyone acknowledges they are usually right. Reliability is fine.
The problem is in the other two terms and it is invisible from inside.

Self-orientation reads high. In reviews they lead with their preferred solution rather than with the
other team's constraint, they argue past the point of decision, and they have twice said "as I
predicted" after an incident. None of that is malicious and all of it signals that being right
matters more than the outcome.

Intimacy is near zero because they have never said they were unsure about anything. Nobody brings
them a half-formed idea, because the response to half-formed ideas has always been correction.

The changes that move it are small and none involves being more right. Opening with what the other
team is worried about. Saying "I don't know, what do you think?" occasionally and meaning it. Saying
"you were right and I was wrong" in public once.

Six months later they are in the rooms. The technical judgment was never the constraint.

## Limits

It is a consulting framework, and the terms are qualitative. Nobody can measure self-orientation, so
the equation is a diagnostic vocabulary rather than a calculation.

It also assumes trust is earned by behaviour, which is true and incomplete. Trust is also affected by
structural factors — seniority, tenure, group membership, and biases the model has nothing to say
about.

And low self-orientation can be performed. Someone who has learned to appear other-directed while
optimising for themselves is worse than someone visibly self-oriented, because the discovery costs
more.

## Source

Maister, Green and Galford developed the equation in *The Trusted Advisor*, from consulting practice
where the central problem is why clients take some advisers' advice and not others'.

Their observation about the denominator is the durable one: professionals systematically try to
increase trust by demonstrating more expertise, when the binding constraint is almost always how
self-oriented they appear.
