## Statement

Six pages of prose read in silence beats slides, because narrative exposes gaps that bullets hide.

## In plain terms

The **narrative memo** replaces a presentation with a written document, read in the room, in
silence, before discussion. The claim is about what each format forces: prose requires you to
connect the ideas, and bullet points let you list them and leave the connections unstated.

## Decides

Whether a substantial decision should be presented or written.

## Why it holds

Bullets permit incoherence. Four points on a slide can be unrelated, contradictory, or missing the
argument between them, and nothing about the format reveals it — the presenter supplies the
connections verbally, so the gap is invisible on the page and in the room.

Prose does not allow that. Writing "and therefore", "which means", "but this conflicts with" forces
the relationships to be stated, and a relationship that does not exist becomes visible while
writing. The document is a test of the thinking before it is a communication of it.

**Silent reading** solves the other half. Pre-reads sent in advance are not read, so the meeting
starts with a summary delivered badly. Reading in the room, for the first twenty minutes, guarantees
shared information — it is awkward and it works.

It also equalises the discussion. Everyone has the same content at the same depth, so the
conversation is not dominated by whoever prepared or whoever speaks first, and questions are about
substance rather than clarification.

Tufte's argument is the complementary one from the other side: the slide format's low resolution
and enforced hierarchy actively degrade the presentation of evidence, and his Columbia analysis is
the strongest available case that the medium changed the conclusion.

## Example

A proposal to rebuild the ingestion pipeline, presented two ways.

The deck has eleven slides: "current pain points" lists five bullets, "proposed architecture" is a
diagram, "benefits" lists four, and "risks" lists three — one of which is "migration complexity".

Every slide is defensible, and the argument connecting them is entirely in the presenter's head.
Nobody notices that two of the pain points are not addressed by the proposed architecture, or that
"migration complexity" is a phrase standing in for an unanswered question about dual-writing.

The memo version cannot hide either. Writing "the proposed architecture addresses pain points one,
three and four; points two and five are not addressed, and here is why that is acceptable" is
forced by the prose — and drafting it is where the author discovers that they had no answer for
point five.

The migration section has the same effect. "Migration complexity" as a bullet is a risk; as a
paragraph it has to say what the complexity is, and writing it surfaces the dual-write question in
week one rather than in month three.

The meeting is twenty minutes of silence and thirty minutes of discussion that starts from shared
information — and the two hardest questions were already answered in the document because writing it
raised them.

## Limits

It is expensive. A good six-page memo takes days, and applying the format to routine updates is a
tax that will be paid in worse memos rather than in better decisions.

The format also suits certain content. Visual comparisons, architecture diagrams, data with shape,
and live demonstration are all genuinely better shown — Tufte's objection is to bullets rather than
to visuals, and a memo with no diagram is not automatically superior.

Silent reading is culturally difficult to introduce. It feels strange, it needs consistent
enforcement, and a half-adopted version — where some people skim and the meeting proceeds as though
everyone read — is worse than a pre-read.

## Source

Amazon replaced presentations with six-page narrative memos in 2004, and Bezos described the
practice publicly in the 2018 shareholder letter, including the detail that a great memo takes a
week or more and that the silent reading exists because pre-reads do not get read.

Tufte's essay supplies the independent argument against the alternative, analysing how the
PowerPoint format contributed to the presentation of the Columbia shuttle damage assessment — the
most-cited case of a medium obscuring an argument.
