## Statement

Name when it happened, what was done, and what followed — never the character trait you inferred.

## In plain terms

**SBI** — situation, behaviour, impact — is the structure that makes feedback usable. Anchor it to a
specific moment, describe what was observably done without adjectives, and say what followed. The
thing you leave out is the inference about what kind of person they are, which is the part that
cannot be acted on.

## Decides

How to phrase feedback so it produces a change rather than a defence.

## Why it holds

Each part removes a specific way feedback fails.

**Situation** anchors it in one recoverable event. "In yesterday's review" makes it about a moment
both people remember, rather than a general pattern the recipient will dispute — and disputing the
pattern is how most feedback conversations avoid the content.

**Behaviour** is what was observably done, with no adjectives. "You interrupted twice" is checkable;
"you were dismissive" is an interpretation, and interpretations are what people argue with. The
recipient frequently did not experience themselves as dismissive, so the adjective is the thing that
turns the conversation into a dispute about characterisation.

**Impact** is what followed, and it is what makes the feedback matter. Behaviour with no consequence
reads as a preference; naming the effect — she stopped contributing, the risk went undiscussed — is
what converts it from taste into information.

The reason the structure works is that each clause is hard to deny. Once the facts are agreed, the
conversation is about what to do differently rather than about whether it happened.

Adding a question at the end is what turns a verdict into a conversation. "What was going on there?"
frequently produces a reason you did not have, and occasionally reverses the feedback.

## Example

Someone cut a colleague off twice in a design review, and she stopped contributing.

The character version: "You were pretty dismissive in that review — you can come across as
arrogant." It is a verdict about who he is, he did not feel dismissive, and the next twenty minutes
are about whether the characterisation is fair.

The SBI version: "In yesterday's review, you cut Priya off twice while she was making the reporting
point. She didn't say anything else for the rest of the meeting, and the reporting risk never got
discussed."

Three clauses, each a fact. He did interrupt twice, she did go quiet, the risk did go undiscussed —
so the conversation starts from agreement rather than from a dispute.

Then the question. "What was going on there?" produces something nobody knew: he had been asked to
keep the meeting to thirty minutes and was watching the clock.

That changes the fix. "Be less dismissive" was never the right correction; "say the time constraint
out loud" is, and it is available only because the structure kept the conversation on what happened.

## Limits

It is a structure for the opening, not a script for the whole conversation. Delivered mechanically —
three clauses and a pause — it reads as a technique being applied to someone, which is its own
problem.

It also handles behavioural feedback specifically. Feedback about the quality of work, about
strategic judgment, or about a pattern across months does not fit the single-situation frame, and
forcing it produces something artificially narrow.

And it does not fix timing or relationship. SBI delivered a month late, in public, or in a
relationship with no history of feedback lands badly however well it is structured.

## Source

The model comes from the Center for Creative Leadership, developed for leadership programmes and
now the most widely taught feedback structure in corporate training.

Its usefulness in engineering comes from the same property that makes it work elsewhere: it is a
forcing function for separating observation from interpretation, which is the distinction Stone,
Patton and Heen identify as the root of most difficult conversations going badly.
